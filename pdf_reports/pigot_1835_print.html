Pigot & Co.’s National Commercial Directory for 1835

Borough, Port & Ancient Market Town of Newport (with Caerleon)

Historical Topographical Survey & Commercial Statistics (1835)

NEWPORT is an ancient market town, seaport, and borough, situated in the hundred of Wentlooge, 148 miles from London, 24 from Bristol, and 12 from Cardiff. It stands on the right bank of the river Usk, over which is an elegant stone bridge of five arches, built by David Edwards (son of the celebrated architect of Pontypridd). The town has grown with prodigious rapidity owing to the Monmouthshire Canal and Sirhowy Tramroad bringing down vast quantities of coal and iron from the hills. The population in 1801 was 1,423; in 1811, 3,025; in 1821, 4,001; and in 1831, 7,062 inhabitants (exclusive of Caerleon); these returns proving how rapidly the population has increased within a few years. CAERLEON is an ancient market town, in the parish of Llangattock, and hundred of Usk, three miles from Newport, seated on the Usk. This town was once the metropolis of Wales, and for extent next to London; it was then the third city in Britain, having an archbishop’s see from the establishment of Christianity in this island. In the time of the Romans it was their principal station; and, according to tradition, the renowned King Arthur here kept his court, and instituted that celebrated order of knighthood, styled 'the order of the round table.' The remains of several antiquities are still found in and around the place—such as a part of the wall that surrounded it, the site of a Roman amphitheatre, and part of a fosse; remains of Roman armour, coins, stone coffins, sepulchral stones, rings, &c. are often dug up here. The walls of Caerleon were built by the Romans with brick, and it is affirmed that they were three miles in circumference. There were also three churches, exclusive of the University, and many elegant structures, besides baths; it is said formerly to have extended to St. Julian’s, and was a place of considerable strength in the reign of Henry II; for the valiant Iorwerth ap Owen ap Caradock, lord of Caerleon, defended it a long time against the King’s forces, though at last vanquished and deprived of his seignorial territory. The name of this ancient and interesting city signifies 'the fortress of Lleon upon Usk,' being so designated from an ancient British king; and not, as Camden states, who says its name is derived from the circumstance of a Roman legion being stationed here. The suburb still bears the classical appellation of ULTRA PONTEM. Giraldus Cambrensis, who, in the fourteenth century, wrote an account of Caerleon, says—'we still see, both within and without the walls, subterraneous buildings, aqueducts, vaulted caverns, and stoves so excellently contrived as to convey their heat through secret and imperceptible pores.' The principal works here are for rolling iron into sheets, and coating them with tin; some of these are very extensive establishments. The places of worship are, one church, and a chapel each for baptists and Wesleyan methodists; the Rev. David Phillips is the present officiating minister of the church. The living of Llangattock is a vicarage, in the gift of the prebends of Llandaff. The free school here was founded and endowed, in 1724, by Charles Williams, Esq. for clothing and educating twenty-five boys, and the like number of girls. The other principal charity is an alms-house for aged widows. The weekly market is held on Thursday, and another every alternate Monday for cattle, sheep, horses, pigs, &c. There are four annual fairs, namely, the third Wednesday in February, May 1st, July 20th, and September 21st. The parish of Llangattock contained, in 1821, 1,360 inhabitants, and in 1831, 1,362; of which last number 1,071 were returned for Caerleon. POST OFFICE, High street, NEWPORT.—George Griffiths, Post Master.—Letters from London, Bristol, &c. arrive every afternoon at a quarter past one, and are despatched every afternoon at a quarter before twelve.—Letters from Birmingham, Liverpool, Manchester, Chepstow, &c. arrive every afternoon at five minutes past twelve, and are despatched every afternoon at a quarter past one.—Letters from Abergavenny, Brecon, Pontypool, Usk, and Crickhowell arrive every forenoon at a quarter past eleven, and are despatched every afternoon at two.—Letters from South Wales arrive every day at five minutes before eleven, and are despatched every afternoon at half-past two.—Letters from Tredegar, &c. arrive every morning at half-past ten, and are despatched every afternoon at half-past two.
